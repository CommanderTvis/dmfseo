In brief: Dunes, salt marshes and open beaches are where many North Sea birds breed, rest and feed. They are easily damaged by trampling and disturbance, especially between April and July. You protect them by staying on marked paths, keeping out of fenced breeding areas, keeping dogs on a lead and taking your litter home. This page explains why, with sources, using the Lower Saxony Wadden Sea National Park as the example.

Why coastal habitats matter

The German North Sea coast is not one habitat but a mosaic: open beaches, dunes behind them, salt marshes on the sheltered side of islands and on the mainland, and the tidal flats of the Wadden Sea. Each part does a different job for birds, and several of them also protect people from storm surges. Losing or disturbing one part affects the birds that depend on it, even when the rest of the coast looks untouched.

Dunes

Dunes only form because grasses such as marram grass bind the loose sand with long roots and keep growing up through it. A dune ages from the first foredune to white, grey and brown dune. Where a chain of protective dunes exists, no dike is needed. Rare plants live only here, and shelduck and curlew like to breed in the dunes.

Salt marshes

Salt marshes grow where tides leave sediment on a flat coast; parts of them are flooded with salt water up to twice a day. They shield dikes from storm surges and store carbon in their soil. Hundreds of thousands of migrating birds rest here in spring and autumn, and oystercatcher, redshank, terns and gulls breed here.

Open beaches

Little terns, ringed plovers and Kentish plovers build no real nest. They lay their eggs in a shallow scrape on bare sand or shell gravel, where the eggs are almost invisible. That camouflage protects them from predators, but it also means people can step on them without noticing.

What disturbance does to beach-nesting birds

When a person, a dog or a kite comes too close, the adult birds leave the nest. Unprotected eggs and chicks then cool down or overheat within a short time. This usually happens without the visitor noticing anything, because the birds are small and well camouflaged. The main breeding season of beach-nesting birds in the Lower Saxony Wadden Sea runs from April to July.

Dogs make a large difference. A study of incubating Kentish plovers on Spanish beaches (Gómez-Serrano 2020) found that walkers with dogs flushed the birds from their nests 80% of the time on paths and 93.8% of the time in the dunes. For walkers without dogs the figures were 12.9% and 47.6%. Keeping to paths and keeping dogs on a lead are therefore the two most effective things a visitor can do.

How protection works: zones in the Wadden Sea National Park

The Lower Saxony Wadden Sea National Park, founded in 1986, is divided into zones. Rules differ between national parks and change over time, so always follow the signs and the official information on site.

How to visit the coast responsibly

  1. Use the marked dune crossings and boardwalks. Do not sit, rest or camp in the dunes, and do not trample the grasses on the foredunes and dune edges.
  2. Stay outside fenced or signed breeding areas. They are marked for the whole breeding season; walk around them, not through them.
  3. Keep dogs on a lead. In the quiet and intermediate zones of the Lower Saxony park this is required all year.
  4. Read the birds' warning signs. If birds call loudly, dive at you or pretend to have a broken wing, you are too close to eggs or chicks. Move on calmly.
  5. Watch your step on bare sand and shell gravel between April and July, and fly kites only in marked areas, never over breeding grounds.
  6. Look, don't pick. Smelling and admiring dune and salt-marsh plants is fine; picking or pulling them out is not.
  7. Watch and photograph from a distance. Binoculars and a zoom lens let you see more than the birds will allow if you walk closer.
  8. Take every piece of litter home, including food scraps, and do not feed wild birds. Why this matters: how plastic pollution affects North Sea seabirds.
  9. Join a guided tour. National park information centres and rangers offer guided walks through dunes, salt marshes and tidal flats, including into areas you may not enter alone.

Join an established beach clean-up

Grohnpetrel is a student project and does not organise clean-ups. If you want to collect litter with others, join an established initiative. The International Coastal Cleanup Day takes place every September and has been run internationally by the US organisation Ocean Conservancy for more than 30 years. In Germany, NABU has taken part since 2010 through its campaign “Meere ohne Plastik”; NABU and NAJU groups organise collections at coasts, rivers and lakes, and you can also register your own clean-up and report what you found.
