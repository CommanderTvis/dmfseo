In brief: This guide introduces six seabirds and coastal birds of the North Sea region, from the gannet, with wings up to 1.8 m across, to the storm petrel, just 14–18 cm long. Two of them, the Atlantic puffin and the black-legged kittiwake, are classed as Vulnerable worldwide. Every fact links to its source.

Species profiles: habitat, threats and how to help

For each bird: where it lives, what threatens it and what you can do. Facts come from the species pages and BirdLife International; photos are openly licensed from Wikimedia Commons.

Northern gannet

Morus bassanus · IUCN: Least Concern

One of the biggest seabirds of the North Atlantic, with wings up to 1.8 m across. It dives head-first into the sea at up to about 100 km/h.

  • Habitat: Dense colonies on sea cliffs and rocky islands; in Germany only on Helgoland, where it has bred again since 1991.
  • Threats: Bird flu (in 2022 H5N1 hit about 40 colonies), bycatch in fishing gear, collisions with offshore wind turbines, and plastic and nets in nests.
  • How to help: Watch colonies from viewpoints only, and never leave fishing line or nets on the coast.

Atlantic puffin

Fratercula arctica · IUCN: Vulnerable

A small auk that spends autumn and winter far out on the open ocean and comes to the coast only to breed.

  • Habitat: Burrows in grassy cliff-top colonies, for example in Iceland, Norway, the Faroe Islands, Britain and Ireland.
  • Threats: Falling stocks of small fish such as sandeels, climate change, rats and cats on nesting islands, oil pollution and fishing nets.
  • How to help: Keep to marked paths at colonies and keep dogs on a lead, so burrows and birds are not disturbed.

Black-legged kittiwake

Rissa tridactyla · IUCN: Vulnerable

A small, ocean-going gull named after its shrill call, “kitti-wa-ake”. Unlike many gulls, it does not feed at rubbish dumps.

  • Habitat: Narrow ledges on sheer sea cliffs, including Helgoland’s bird cliff; some colonies nest on buildings.
  • Threats: Fewer sandeels because of fishing and warmer seas; numbers have fallen by about 40% over three generations.
  • How to help: Do not disturb cliff colonies: watch from a distance and keep noise down.

Common tern

Sterna hirundo · IUCN: Least Concern

A slender, fork-tailed seabird that catches small fish by diving from 1–6 m and spends the winter off western and southern Africa.

  • Habitat: Colonies on beaches, dunes, salt marshes and islands, on flat ground with little vegetation.
  • Threats: Disturbance by people, dogs, vehicles and boats; flooding of low nests; rats and large gulls.
  • How to help: Stay outside fenced breeding areas from April to July and keep dogs on a lead.

Eurasian oystercatcher

Haematopus ostralegus · IUCN: Near Threatened

A black-and-white shorebird with a long orange-red bill. Despite its name, oysters are not a big part of its diet.

  • Habitat: Open coasts, mudflats and salt marshes; it nests in a bare scrape on sand or pebbles.
  • Threats: Disturbance of nests on open ground, where eggs and chicks are easy to step on; long-term decline in the Wadden Sea.
  • How to help: Watch your step on beaches in spring and keep to marked paths in salt marshes.

European storm petrel

Hydrobates pelagicus · IUCN: Least Concern

One of Europe’s smallest seabirds, just 14–18 cm long, and the real petrel behind our project’s name.

  • Habitat: Crevices and burrows on remote Atlantic and Mediterranean islands; it visits its colony only at night.
  • Threats: Rats, cats and mink on nesting islands, and predators such as skuas and large gulls.
  • How to help: Never bring pets to seabird islands, and support organisations that keep nesting islands free of rats.

Is there a seabird called Grohnpetrel?

No. No bird species is called Grohnpetrel: the word is the name of our fictional education project (see What is Grohnpetrel?). The second half of the name, petrel, does refer to real seabirds. In this guide, the European storm petrel is the petrel you can meet in the North Sea region. The northern fulmar, which belongs to the same order of tube-nosed seabirds, is the bird scientists use to measure plastic pollution in the North Sea (see How plastic pollution affects North Sea seabirds).

Where to see seabirds in Germany

Helgoland, an island in the German Bight, has Germany’s only bird cliff, the Lummenfelsen. About 10,000 pairs nest there, including common guillemots, northern gannets, black-legged kittiwakes, razorbills and northern fulmars (Helgoland tourism office). Along the mainland coast, the Wadden Sea, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2009, is a key area for shorebirds such as the oystercatcher and for nesting terns.

What threatens these birds

The same pressures come up again and again: less food as small fish such as sandeels decline, climate change, bycatch in fishing gear, introduced predators on nesting islands, pollution, disturbance of nests and, since 2022, bird flu. Read more in Seabird Conservation: Threats and Challenges.
