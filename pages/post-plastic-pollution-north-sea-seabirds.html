In brief: Plastic harms North Sea seabirds in two main ways: birds swallow it, and they get caught in it. In 2019–2023, 48% of northern fulmars found dead on the German North Sea coast had more than 0.1 g of plastic in their stomachs; the international target is at most 10% (German Environment Agency). Northern gannets build nests with fishing rope and nets, and some die tangled in them. This guide explains the evidence and what visitors to the coast can do.

Two ways plastic harms seabirds

  • Swallowing it. Seabirds that feed at the sea surface can take floating plastic for food. Plastic in the stomach can take up space meant for food, and sharp or large pieces can injure the gut.
  • Getting caught in it. Lost fishing nets, ropes and lines can wrap around wings, legs and necks. Birds that collect such material for their nests can trap themselves or their chicks.

The northern fulmar: the North Sea’s plastic indicator

The northern fulmar (Fulmarus glacialis) is a seabird of the petrel family that feeds at the sea surface and rarely brings up what it swallows. That makes it a useful indicator: the plastic in its stomach shows how much plastic floats where it feeds. Countries around the North Sea use it in a common monitoring programme under OSPAR, the convention for the protection of the North-East Atlantic (OSPAR assessment).

The rule is simple. Researchers count the share of fulmars found dead on beaches whose stomachs hold more than 0.1 g of plastic. OSPAR’s target is that this share should be at most 10%, a level derived from fulmars in the relatively clean Canadian Arctic.

On the German North Sea coast the latest five-year average (2019–2023) is 48%, and 89% of the 93 birds examined had at least some plastic in their stomachs. Since the studies began, 88% to 97% of beached fulmars have contained plastic. The amount has declined slightly in recent years, but the German Environment Agency expects the target to be reached only in the long term, because plastic keeps entering the sea and breaks down very slowly (German Environment Agency, indicator “Plastic waste in the North Sea”).

Northern gannets: nests made of fishing nets

Germany’s only colony of northern gannets (Morus bassanus) is on the cliffs of Helgoland, where the birds have bred since 1991; NABU describes more than a thousand pairs raising young there each year. Gannets build their nests from seaweed and other material they collect at sea, and today that often includes rope and net pieces (NABU: Basstölpel).

A study of 29 gannet colonies across the species’ range checked 7,280 nests and found debris in 46% of them, mostly thread-like plastic that probably comes from fishing. Colonies in areas with more fishing activity had more debris in their nests (O’Hanlon et al. 2019, Environmental Pollution).

This material can kill. On Helgoland, researchers recorded gannets entangled in net fragments as early as 1976–1985: about 29% of the observed deaths in that period, including birds rescued alive, were attributed to plastic debris and fishing gear (Schrey & Vauk 1987, Marine Pollution Bulletin). NABU notes that many adult and young gannets still die after becoming entangled in nets and plastic used as nesting material.

A global problem, not only a North Sea one

A global analysis of studies from 1962 to 2012 found plastic in the stomachs of 80 of 135 seabird species examined (59%). The authors estimated that about 90% of individual seabirds would contain plastic if the studies were repeated today, and predicted that plastic ingestion would reach 99% of all seabird species by 2050 if nothing changes (Wilcox, Van Sebille & Hardesty 2015, PNAS).

What you can do on the coast

  • Take all your rubbish home, including small items such as bottle caps, cigarette filters and bits of fishing line.
  • If you fish, collect lost line, hooks and net pieces you find instead of leaving them on the beach.
  • Join a beach clean organised by a recognised local group, so the litter is collected and recorded safely.
  • If you see an entangled or injured bird, keep your distance and contact a local wildlife rescue or the island or park authority instead of trying to free it yourself.
  • More everyday steps are in our guide Take action: how to help protect seabirds.
