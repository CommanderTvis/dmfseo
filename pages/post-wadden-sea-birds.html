In brief: The Wadden Sea, shared by the Netherlands, Germany and Denmark, is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2009. On average 10 to 12 million migratory birds pass through it every year, and up to 6.1 million can be there at the same time (UNESCO). Long-term counts show some species increasing and others, such as the Eurasian oystercatcher, declining.

The Wadden Sea lies on the North Sea coast, close to where the Weser from Bremen-Grohn reaches the sea. It is the landscape behind several birds in our seabird guide. We use this real ecosystem as an educational example; Grohnpetrel does no conservation work there. The Wadden Sea is protected by the Netherlands, Germany and Denmark together, through the Trilateral Wadden Sea Cooperation.

What makes the Wadden Sea special

  • Size and shape: a World Heritage property of 1,143,403 ha stretching from the Netherlands through Germany to Denmark; the Danish part, added later, enlarged it by about 1,600 km².
  • Unique landscape: UNESCO calls it the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world. Twice a day the tide uncovers flats full of worms, shellfish and other small animals.
  • Rich life: about 2,300 species of plants and animals live in the salt marshes and a further 2,700 in the marine and brackish areas, and 30 species of birds breed there.

Three coastal habitats of the Wadden Sea

UNESCO lists mudflats, salt marshes, beaches and dunes among the many transitional habitats between land and sea that make the Wadden Sea so rich in life. Each one plays a different part for birds (Common Wadden Sea Secretariat).

Tidal mudflats

Twice a day the falling tide uncovers the flats, which make up more than half of the Wadden Sea’s tidal area. They look empty, but microscopic algae coat the surface and the sediment hides worms, shellfish, snails and shrimp. Lugworms alone number about one billion, the largest worm population in the world.

  • Why birds need it: At low tide, waders such as the dunlin and the oystercatcher feed on these small animals.
  • Conservation issue: Mudflat birds that eat shellfish and other bottom-living animals are declining, above all in the German Wadden Sea (trilateral report, 2025).

Salt marshes

Salt marshes are open grasslands between land and sea that the tide floods from time to time. Each flood leaves a thin layer of sediment, so the marsh slowly grows upwards. Only about 45 kinds of flowering plants grow there, yet with insects, spiders and other small animals the Wadden Sea’s salt marshes hold about 2,300 species.

  • Why birds need it: About 100 bird species feed and rest in the salt marshes, and waders such as the oystercatcher and the redshank breed there.
  • Conservation issue: The nests lie on the ground between the grasses, so leaving the paths in spring and early summer can destroy them.

Coastal dunes and beaches

On the barrier islands, wind blows sand off the dry beach and pioneer plants such as marram grass trap it, so dunes build up step by step. Beaches and dunes work as one system and give birds places to feed, nest and rest.

  • Why birds need it: The Kentish plover and the little tern, both on the Red List, lay their eggs on open beaches.
  • Conservation issue: They choose the same beaches that people like most, so a single walker or dog can keep the parents away from the eggs.
  • Responsible visiting: Use boardwalks like this one on Juist and stay out of fenced areas.

How coastal habitats support wildlife

The four steps below show how the habitats above, the birds and visitors are connected.

  1. Step 1The tide uncovers the flatsTwice a day the water drops and exposes mudflats full of worms, shellfish and shrimp.
  2. Step 2Birds feed and restMigratory birds refuel on this food at low tide and gather on salt marshes, beaches and sandbanks at high tide.
  3. Step 3Protection keeps food and quietNational parks protect the flats, marshes and dunes. UNESCO names food and a low level of disturbance as the keys to the Wadden Sea’s role for birds.
  4. Step 4Visitors make the differenceKeeping your distance, staying on paths and keeping dogs on a lead lets birds spend their energy on feeding and breeding, not on fleeing.

A refuelling stop on the flyway

For migratory birds the Wadden Sea is a service station on the East Atlantic Flyway, the route between Arctic breeding grounds and wintering areas in Western Europe and Africa. Birds stop to rest and feed on the exposed flats before flying on. UNESCO counts up to 6.1 million birds present at the same time and an average of 10 to 12 million passing through each year (UNESCO World Heritage List: Wadden Sea).

Why this ecosystem matters

  • 43 speciesMore than 1% of the flyway population of 43 bird species uses the Wadden Sea, the threshold for a wetland of international importance under the Ramsar Convention.
  • Almost allAlmost the entire population of the dark-bellied brent goose and the entire European and West-Russian population of the dunlin use the area.
  • Over 25%More than a quarter of the European populations of Eurasian spoonbill, pied avocet, gull-billed tern and sandwich tern breed in the Wadden Sea region.
  • Late summerAlmost all shelducks of Northern and Western Europe moult in the least disturbed parts of the Wadden Sea.

Source: Common Wadden Sea Secretariat, “Richly diverse” (accessed 16 November 2026). UNESCO adds that enough food and a low level of disturbance are essential for this role.

Which birds are doing well, and which are not

The Netherlands, Germany and Denmark count migratory and wintering waterbirds together. A report published in 2025 covers 36 years, from 1987/1988 to 2022/2023 (Trilateral Expert Group Migratory Birds, 2025):

The report links part of the pattern to food: birds of the intertidal mudflats that feed on bottom-living animals, especially shellfish-eaters, are declining, above all in the German Wadden Sea.

How to visit without disturbing birds

  • Stay on marked paths and respect closed areas, especially in the breeding season.
  • Keep a wide distance from resting flocks, above all around high tide when birds gather on small dry areas and cannot easily move away.
  • Keep dogs on a lead where birds rest or breed.
  • Only walk on the mudflats with a licensed guide: the tide comes in fast.
  • Take your litter home; why that matters is explained in how plastic pollution affects North Sea seabirds.
