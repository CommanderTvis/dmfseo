In brief: The Wadden Sea, shared by the Netherlands, Germany and Denmark, is the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2009. On average 10 to 12 million migratory birds pass through it every year, and up to 6.1 million can be there at the same time (UNESCO). Long-term counts show some species increasing and others, such as the Eurasian oystercatcher, declining.

The Wadden Sea lies on the North Sea coast, close to where the Weser from Bremen-Grohn reaches the sea. It is the landscape behind several birds in our seabird guide. What makes the Wadden Sea special

  • Size and shape: a World Heritage property of 1,143,403 ha stretching from the Netherlands through Germany to Denmark; the Danish part, added later, enlarged it by about 1,600 km².
  • Unique landscape: UNESCO calls it the largest unbroken system of intertidal sand and mud flats in the world. Twice a day the tide uncovers flats full of worms, shellfish and other small animals.
  • Rich life: about 2,300 species of plants and animals live in the salt marshes and a further 2,700 in the marine and brackish areas, and 30 species of birds breed there.

A refuelling stop on the flyway

For migratory birds the Wadden Sea is a service station on the East Atlantic Flyway, the route between Arctic breeding grounds and wintering areas in Western Europe and Africa. Birds stop to rest and feed on the exposed flats before flying on. UNESCO counts up to 6.1 million birds present at the same time and an average of 10 to 12 million passing through each year (UNESCO World Heritage List: Wadden Sea).

Which birds are doing well, and which are not

The Netherlands, Germany and Denmark count migratory and wintering waterbirds together. A report published in 2025 covers 36 years, from 1987/1988 to 2022/2023 (Trilateral Expert Group Migratory Birds, 2025):

The report links part of the pattern to food: birds of the intertidal mudflats that feed on bottom-living animals, especially shellfish-eaters, are declining, above all in the German Wadden Sea.

How to visit without disturbing birds

  • Stay on marked paths and respect closed areas, especially in the breeding season.
  • Keep a wide distance from resting flocks, above all around high tide when birds gather on small dry areas and cannot easily move away.
  • Keep dogs on a lead where birds rest or breed.
  • Only walk on the mudflats with a licensed guide: the tide comes in fast.
  • Take your litter home; why that matters is explained in how plastic pollution affects North Sea seabirds.
